Week 1 ended with a photon being absorbed. Week 2 asks: absorbed by *what*, and what moves afterwards?

Silicon atoms hold hands. Each one shares its 4 outer electrons with 4 neighbours, so in a perfect, cold crystal every electron is locked in a bond and nothing can carry a current. Kick one electron out of its bond, with heat or with a photon, and you get **two** mobile charges: the <Term id="free-electron" /> itself, and the empty spot it left behind, the <Term id="hole" />. Neighbouring bond electrons hop into the hole one after another, so the hole wanders through the crystal like a positive charge.

At room temperature only very few bonds are broken: about 10¹⁰ per cm³, out of 5 × 10²² atoms. That is one free electron per **five trillion** atoms, which is why pure silicon is a poor conductor. <Term id="doping">Doping</Term> changes that dramatically. Replace one in every 500 000 silicon atoms by phosphorus, which brings a fifth electron that has no bond to sit in, and the electron density jumps ten million times. Boron, with one electron too few, does the same for holes.

To keep track of all this we draw **energy**, not positions: bonded electrons live in the <Term id="valence-band" />, freed electrons in the <Term id="conduction-band" />, with the <Term id="bandgap" /> in between. One energy, the <Term id="fermi-level" />, summarises the whole electron population like a water level: high water (close to the conduction band) means many free electrons, low water means many holes.

Light splits that single water level in two. That split, the quasi-Fermi-level splitting, is the energy each electron can deliver to a circuit. Keep it in mind: it returns as the cell voltage in week 5.
