Week 2 counted the electrons and holes. Week 3 lets them move, appear and disappear.

Carriers in a crystal never sit still: they rattle around at about 10⁷ cm/s, bouncing off vibrating atoms. On its own this random motion goes nowhere, like a crowd milling around a square. Two things turn it into a net flow, a current. An **electric field** pushes electrons one way and holes the other: <Term id="drift" />. A **difference in crowding** makes more carriers wander from the busy side to the quiet side than back: <Term id="diffusion" />. In a solar cell under light, diffusion does most of the work outside the junction.

Light keeps creating electron–hole pairs (<Term id="generation" />), most of them close to the front surface. But every pair is on borrowed time. Sooner or later the electron finds a hole and they <Term id="recombination">recombine</Term>, giving their energy back as heat or light. Defects, impurities and the surface are where this happens most.

Two numbers summarise the race. The <Term id="minority-carrier-lifetime" /> τ is how long a carrier survives on average. The <Term id="diffusion-length" /> $L = \sqrt{D\tau}$ is how far it wanders in that time. If $L$ is much longer than the distance to the junction, almost every photogenerated carrier is collected; if it is shorter, most are lost before they get there.

The <Term id="continuity-equation" /> is the bookkeeping that ties it all together: change = inflow − outflow + generation − recombination. Solve it and you get the carrier profile inside a wafer, which is exactly what the instruction exercises ask.
